## 001 MAX effect long-short (discovery: scored, Sharpe -0.11, t -0.4, DSR 0.07)
Category: research.
Hypothesis: Stocks with the largest single-day return over the past month are lottery tickets that skew-loving investors overpay for (Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw, "Maxing out", JFE); long the lowest-MAX decile, short the highest, monthly, dollar-neutral.
Case: The paper finds a large, robust spread, and the mechanism (preference for positive skew among retail and constrained investors, plus costly shorting of the volatile tail) does not need any data the judge lacks. The strongest reason it might not work here: the universe is the 1,000 most liquid stocks, where the lottery stocks the paper's spread comes from (small, cheap, illiquid) are mostly absent, and the short leg is a concentrated high-volatility basket that gets squeezed in rallies. A dollar-neutral book with beta near -0.4 is also a bet against the market that can swamp the anomaly.
Result: Sharpe -0.11 (gross 0.02), opposite to expected sign but not significant; beta -0.41, alpha Sharpe 0.20 (below noise bar 0.34). Costs 1.5%/yr at 17x turnover.
Learned: In the liquid universe the raw MAX spread is mostly a short-beta position; once beta is accounted for there is a small positive alpha, not significant. A beta-neutral construction would be the substantive change, not a different lookback. Not pursued further this session.

## 002 return seasonality (discovery: scored, Sharpe -0.70, t -2.3, DSR 0.00)
Category: research.
Hypothesis: A stock's return in the coming month resembles its average return in the same month of the previous five years (Heston & Sadka, "Seasonality in the cross-section of stock returns", JFE). Long top decile, short bottom decile of that average, monthly, dollar-neutral.
Case: The effect is documented as persisting for many annual lags and is not explained by size, value or momentum, which suggests a recurring cause (annual earnings and dividend calendars, fiscal-year-end flows, tax and window-dressing trades) rather than a one-off. Being dollar-neutral and sorted on a signal unrelated to volatility, it should have little market beta. The strongest reason it might not work: each lag is a single noisy monthly return, so even averaged over five years the signal is weak, and in the most liquid stocks the recurring flows are exactly what arbitrageurs already front-run. Using 252 trading days as a "year" also blurs the calendar alignment slightly.
Result: Sharpe -0.70 (gross -0.42), significantly OPPOSITE to the expected sign; beta 0.03, alpha Sharpe -0.74; one extra day of lag barely changes it (-0.76). Exposure averaged 0.81, so part of the block had too little history (fewer than three annual lags) and sat partly in cash.
Learned: In this liquid universe and block, stocks that did well in the same month of past years did worse the next month, not better. Recorded, not flipped. A reversed version would need its own argued mechanism (e.g. the annual lags at 252 days overlap the window where the 12-month reversal / long-term mean reversion lives) and would count as a new trial; I did not run it.

## 003 high-volume return premium (discovery: scored, Sharpe -0.59, t -1.9, DSR 0.00)
Category: research.
Hypothesis: Stocks with an unusually high trading volume last week (5-day average over the prior 50-day average) outperform over the next month, and unusually quiet ones underperform (Gervais, Kaniel & Mingelgrin, "The high-volume return premium", JF). Long top decile, short bottom decile, monthly, dollar-neutral.
Case: The visibility mechanism is simple and does not depend on firm size: a volume shock draws new buyers' attention, and because shorting is costlier than buying, the extra attention mostly adds demand. The paper's effect holds after controlling for the formation-period return, so it is not just reversal. The strongest reason it might not work: volume shocks in large liquid stocks are mostly news (earnings, guidance), and their aftermath is dominated by drift or reversal in the news direction, which this signal ignores; the short leg (quiet stocks) is also low-volatility and may earn the low-vol premium instead of underperforming.
Result: Sharpe -0.59 (gross -0.19), opposite to expected sign, t -1.9 (just short of significant); beta 0.02, alpha Sharpe -0.63. Costs 1.9%/yr at 21x turnover turn a small gross loss into a larger net one. One extra day of lag makes it less negative (-0.34), so part of the loss sits in the first day after the signal (consistent with high-volume stocks having just moved and then reversing).
Learned: The premium does not appear in the liquid universe over this block; if anything volume shocks are followed by underperformance, probably because the shock carries a short-term price move that reverses. A version that conditions on a small formation-period return (isolating volume from price) would be the substantive test; not run.

## 004 RSI(2) pullback in uptrend (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.32, t 1.0, DSR 0.08)
Category: popular-online.
Hypothesis: The widely shared Connors RSI(2) rule: buy a stock trading above its 200-day average when its 2-day RSI drops below 10; sell when it closes above its 5-day average. Long-only, daily, at most 10% per position.
Case: Short-horizon reversal in liquid stocks is one of the better-documented effects (the weekly-reversal and buy-the-dip baselines are both positive here), and the trend filter should keep it away from stocks in genuine decline, where dips keep going. The exit is quick, so capital is only tied up during the bounce. The strongest reasons it might not work: the rule trades often, so costs at next-open execution can eat a bounce that mostly happens overnight after the signal, and capping positions at 10% leaves the book partly in cash on quiet days, diluting returns. Its popularity also means many traders front-run the same signal.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.32 but gross 0.97: costs 13.5%/yr at 188x turnover. Beta 0.84, alpha Sharpe 0.02, so the net result is basically the market. One extra day of lag drops it to 0.05: the bounce lives in the first day.
Learned: The reversal signal is real gross of costs but too fast and too frequently rebalanced to survive next-open execution with costs. Equal-weighting the whole held set every day also adds turnover. A lower-turnover version (fixed per-position size, no daily reweighting, longer hold) would be the substantive change. Rules out short-hold oscillator rules as a cheap edge here.

## 005 MACD bullish state (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.13, t 0.4, DSR 0.03)
Category: popular-online.
Hypothesis: Hold every universe stock whose MACD(12,26) line is above zero and above its 9-day signal line; equal weight, rebalanced weekly (weekly rather than daily, chosen before running, because 004 showed daily oscillator rules die on costs). Long-only.
Case: MACD is the most-cited trend indicator on retail forums. The argument for it is the same as for time-series momentum: news is absorbed slowly, so a stock whose medium-term trend is up and steepening keeps drifting for a while. Requiring both conditions avoids buying a stock merely bouncing inside a downtrend. The strongest reason it might not work: it holds roughly a third to half of the universe at any time, so it is mostly the market with a few weeks' lag, and 12-26 day trends sit right in the horizon where individual stocks show reversal rather than continuation. Expect beta near 1 and the real test to be alpha Sharpe.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.13 (gross 0.31), beta 0.97, alpha Sharpe -0.39; max drawdown -66%, worse than holding everything. Costs 3.8%/yr at 52x turnover. Holds about 336 names.
Learned: As expected, MACD state is the market with lag and extra costs; the timing adds negative alpha. Short-to-medium trend rules on single stocks don't pay in this universe. Never again in this form.

## 006 sell in May (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.58, t 1.9, DSR 0.30)
Category: popular-online.
Hypothesis: Hold the equal-weighted universe from the start of November to the end of April and sit in cash from May to October. Long-only, monthly decisions. The honest test is not the sign (any long equity book is likely positive) but whether the in/out timing earns a Sharpe and alpha above holding all year.
Case: This is a calendar rule over a feature (month of year), not a memory of any particular year, and it is one of the most repeated retail rules. Bouman & Jacobsen ("The Halloween indicator", AER) report it across many countries, which argues against pure data mining, and plausible mechanisms exist (vacation-driven liquidity and risk appetite, year-end flows and bonuses). The strongest reasons it might not work: it is a single binary bet repeated once a year, so a block of about a decade gives only about ten independent observations and the t-stat will be weak almost regardless; and the cash months earn nothing here (no interest modelled), which handicaps the rule against buy-and-hold.
Result: Sharpe 0.58 vs equal-weight benchmark 0.39, at half the exposure (beta 0.50), alpha Sharpe 0.42; return 8.0%/yr, max DD -39%, costs negligible. t 1.9, DSR 0.30, and net Sharpe is below the noise bar (0.73). Doesn't qualify for validation.
Learned: The best risk-adjusted result so far, and robust to an extra day of lag, but it rests on roughly ten annual switches, so it can't be told apart from luck in one block. Worth a human's glance only as a cheap, low-turnover overlay; the evidence here is weak. Not iterated (any tweak of the months would be fishing).

## 007 gap-down reversal candle (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.13, t 0.4, DSR 0.03)
Category: pattern.
Hypothesis: When a stock gaps down at least 3% at the open and then closes above its open (a bullish candle after a gap), buy at the next open and hold five days. Long-only, 2% per position.
Case: Overnight gaps concentrate uninformed liquidity demand (stop-loss orders, market-on-open sells, reaction to headlines before the market can price them), and the intraday recovery is evidence that informed buyers judged the drop too large. Short-term reversal is positive in this universe (both reversal baselines score above zero), and this pattern is a sharper filter for "overreaction already being corrected". The strongest reason it might not work: the gap often carries real news (earnings, downgrades) and post-news drift runs in the gap's direction for weeks; the intraday recovery may already be the whole correction, leaving nothing after the next open. The book is also partly in cash on days with few events.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.13 (gross 0.29), max DD -68%. Average exposure 0.51 yet beta 1.11: gap-downs cluster on market sell-off days, so the book loads up exactly when the market is falling and holds about 42 names. Alpha Sharpe -0.37. Costs 4%/yr.
Learned: Single-stock gap patterns in this universe are mostly a market-timing bet in disguise (buy after market-wide gaps), and that bet loses on risk-adjusted terms. A market-neutral version (hedge with the equal-weighted universe, or require the gap to be idiosyncratic relative to the market's own gap) is the substantive next step; not run.

## 008 relative breakout on volume (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.37, t 1.2, DSR 0.13)
Category: pattern.
Hypothesis: Buy a stock when its price relative to the equal-weighted market closes above its prior 50-day high on volume at least 1.5x its 50-day average; hold 20 days, 2% per position, long-only. Defined relative to the market (a change made before running, from 007's lesson that raw single-stock patterns cluster on market-wide days).
Case: Breakouts on volume are the core of classic chart-reading (Darvas, O'Neil), and the economic story is order splitting: a large investor accumulating a position pushes the stock to new relative highs on heavy volume and keeps buying for weeks. Measuring the breakout relative to the market stops the rule from simply buying everything in a broad rally. The strongest reason it might not work: one-day volume spikes at highs are often news events whose drift is short or already priced at the next open, and 50-day relative highs sit in the horizon (1-3 months) where cross-sectional returns are close to neither momentum nor reversal. The 52-week-high baseline, a slower cousin, only scored 0.35.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.37 (gross 0.47) against benchmark 0.39; beta 1.05, alpha Sharpe 0.02. Always fully invested (about 191 names), so the slot cap binds and it behaves like a high-beta market portfolio. Insensitive to an extra day of lag.
Learned: Volume-confirmed relative breakouts select stocks that do about as well as the market, no better. Combined with the 52-week-high baseline, price-high signals look dead in this universe and block. Never again as a long-only book; only a hedged version could tell a small edge from beta.

## 009 wide-range strong close, hedged (discovery: scored, Sharpe -1.16, t -3.9, DSR 0.00)
Category: pattern.
Hypothesis: On a day when a stock's high-low range is at least twice its 20-day average, it closes up, and the close is in the top 10% of the day's range, buy it for five days and short the equal-weighted universe by the same dollar amount. Market-hedged by construction (a choice made before running, after 007 and 008 showed long-only pattern books are mostly beta).
Case: The closing auction is where index funds and institutions do much of their trading, and a stock that rallies hard and still closes at its high has buyers who were not finished; order-splitting by those buyers predicts continuation over days. The close-location value is a standard chart-reading input (Williams %R, accumulation/distribution) but is rarely tested as a hedged event. The strongest reason it might not work: wide-range up days are often news-driven, and in liquid stocks the news is priced by the next open, so next-open entry may buy the top; short-term reversal (positive in the baselines here) works directly against a one-day continuation bet.
Result: Gross Sharpe -0.07 (no edge either way), net -1.16 with t -3.9: the significant loss is almost entirely costs (3.0%/yr on a book averaging 0.48 gross exposure). Beta -0.01, so the hedge worked. The judge flagged one position-day with a move above +300% (a possible bad print); with gross near zero it doesn't change the reading.
Learned: The pattern itself shows no continuation. The construction was my mistake: resizing a short hedge across all ~1,000 universe names every day as the long side changes generates large turnover in tiny slices. Next time hedge with a slower-moving short (rebalance the hedge weekly, or hedge only the net change) or use a cross-sectional long-short where both legs come from the signal. Recorded as a cost lesson, not evidence that close location reverses.

## 010 composite rank (vol, momentum, MAX) (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.53, t 1.8, DSR 0.09)
Category: numeric.
Hypothesis: Average three cross-sectional percentile ranks (low 60-day volatility, high 12-1 momentum, low 21-day maximum daily return) and hold the top decile of the average, equal weight, monthly, long-only.
Case: Each signal on its own is noisy, but they are only partly correlated, so a rank average should hold stocks that are good on all three at once and diversify away the idiosyncratic misses of each; this is the logic of multi-factor composites in practice. The low-vol baseline (0.52) is the best single-signal baseline here, and momentum and low-MAX should add something different. The strongest reasons it might not work: low volatility and low MAX are highly correlated (both pick calm stocks), so the composite is mostly low-vol with a momentum tilt; momentum scored only 0.34 here and its crashes hit hardest at turning points, which may damage what low-vol earns. With 001 showing low-MAX had no clear alpha, two of three ingredients are untested winners.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.53 (gross 0.59), beta 0.70, alpha Sharpe 0.45, costs 0.9%/yr. About the same as the low-vol baseline (0.52), not better; t 1.8, and well under the noise bar (now 0.93). One extra day of lag costs 0.15 of Sharpe, more than I'd expect for a monthly signal, which suggests part of the return comes from the MAX/short-term part of the composite.
Learned: Adding momentum and low-MAX to low-vol didn't improve on low-vol alone in this block. The composite's alpha is mostly the low-beta tilt. Not worth iterating weights (that would be fishing).

## 011 residual z-score reversal (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.36, t 1.2, DSR 0.03)
Category: numeric.
Hypothesis: Each week, compute each stock's 5-day return minus its 120-day beta times the equal-weighted market's 5-day return, divided by its 5-day volatility; hold the bottom decile of that z-score, equal weight, long-only. An argued improvement on the weekly-reversal baseline (0.39), same holding period and universe.
Case: The baseline's loser decile is dominated by high-beta and high-volatility names in down weeks, so much of its return is beta and volatility timing rather than reversal of idiosyncratic overreaction. Residualising on the market and scaling by volatility is the standard fix in statistical-arbitrage practice (and in work on "residual reversal" showing cleaner, more persistent reversal once factor moves are removed), and it should cut drawdowns in sell-offs. The strongest reason it might not work: weekly turnover near 100% in a long-only book is expensive here (the baseline already pays for it), and a stock with a large idiosyncratic drop is disproportionately one with bad news, where drift, not reversal, follows. Scaling by volatility also tilts toward calm stocks whose bounces are small.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.36 (gross 0.62) vs the raw weekly-reversal baseline's 0.39. Costs 6.7%/yr at 93x turnover. Beta 1.18 and alpha Sharpe 0.02; max DD -65%. One extra day of lag drops it to 0.03.
Learned: Residualising the signal does not residualise the book: a long-only basket of recent idiosyncratic losers still carries beta above 1, and the gross improvement (0.62) is eaten by costs. Weekly reversal in this universe lives in the first day and is a cost problem before it is a signal problem. Next time: a slower or hedged reversal, or none at all.

## 012 breadth regime timing (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.54, t 1.8, DSR 0.10)
Category: market-regime.
Hypothesis: Compute breadth as the share of universe stocks closing above their own 200-day average. At each month-end, hold the equal-weighted universe if breadth is above 50%, otherwise cash. Long-only.
Case: Breadth is a market-wide trend signal built from the panel itself, and time-series momentum at the index level is one of the more robust findings across asset classes (Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen); breadth should detect broad downtrends earlier and with less noise than a single index average because it counts how many stocks are participating. Bear phases also coincide with high volatility, so stepping aside should improve Sharpe even if it gives up some return. The strongest reason it might not work: trend timing on a monthly clock is late at both turns, and the sharpest rebounds come right after breadth bottoms, exactly when the rule is in cash. With roughly a decade in the block, there are only a handful of regime switches, so the result rests on few events. Cash earns nothing here.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.54 vs equal-weight 0.39, max drawdown -27% (the baseline buy-and-hold is far deeper), invested 80% of the time, beta 0.44, alpha Sharpe 0.38, costs negligible. t 1.8, below the noise bar (0.92); DSR 0.10. One extra day of lag improves it (0.66), which on a monthly rule is noise. Two position-days with moves above +300% were flagged (bad-print warning); with ~800 names equally weighted, a single print has little effect, but a human should glance at it.
Learned: Breadth timing does what trend timing usually does: much smaller drawdowns, modestly better Sharpe, few switches, so weak statistics. Not distinguishable from luck in one block. Not iterating the 50% threshold or the 200-day window.

## 013 momentum / low-vol regime switch (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.61, t 2.0, DSR 0.15)
Category: market-regime.
Hypothesis: Build the equal-weighted market from the panel. At each month-end, if its two-year return is positive and its 63-day volatility is below its own two-year median ("calm bull"), hold the 12-1 momentum top decile; otherwise hold the lowest-volatility quintile. Long-only, monthly.
Case: Daniel & Moskowitz show momentum's worst losses are concentrated and forecastable: they follow market declines and occur in high-volatility states, when the loser portfolio (high beta, beaten down) rebounds violently. Switching to low volatility in those states removes the crash exposure and holds the style that tends to protect in stress, while keeping momentum where it historically pays. Both legs are baselines here (momentum 0.34, low-vol 0.52), so this is a clear test of whether regime conditioning adds anything. The strongest reason it might not work: the regime filter has few switches per decade and two thresholds, so its timing is noisy; and low-vol held in a recovering market lags badly exactly when momentum would also have done badly, so the switch may just trade one underperformance for another.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.61, above both ingredients (momentum 0.34, low-vol 0.52), return 10.7%/yr, beta 0.85, alpha Sharpe 0.50, costs 0.6%/yr. But t 2.0 (not above 2), DSR 0.15, and well below the noise bar (0.93), so it doesn't qualify for validation. Max DD -55% shows the switch didn't avoid the worst drawdown. One extra day of lag cuts it to 0.38, a large drop for a monthly rule, which suggests a few switch months near turning points carry much of the result.
Learned: Regime-conditioning momentum is the most promising idea of the session on its face, but the evidence is the kind that fails out of sample: few switches, a large lag sensitivity, and a deep drawdown anyway. Not iterating its thresholds. If a later session wants to test it, it should be one pre-registered run, possibly with a smoother (continuous) blend instead of a hard switch, argued first.

## 014 quiet accumulation (discovery: scored, Sharpe -0.58, t -1.9, DSR 0.00)
Category: novel.
Hypothesis: Among stocks whose 40-day return is in the middle third of the universe (price roughly flat relative to peers), rank by the share of 40-day volume traded on up-close days. Long the top decile, short the bottom decile, dollar-neutral, monthly.
Case: Institutions execute large orders over weeks with algorithms designed to hide them, so the price impact is small but the footprint remains: buying algorithms trade more on upticks and on days the stock is rising, sellers on down days. Conditioning on a flat price separates this footprint from ordinary momentum (where up-volume share is high simply because the stock went up). If the order is unfinished, its remaining part pushes the price in the same direction, and the dollar-neutral construction keeps beta near zero. The strongest reason it might not work: daily up/down classification is a crude proxy for buyer- or seller-initiated volume, and the share is dominated by one or two high-volume news days in the window, which may carry their own drift or reversal unrelated to accumulation. It is also my own idea with no prior evidence, so the prior should be low.
Result: Net Sharpe -0.58 (gross -0.20), opposite to expected sign, t -1.9 (not significant); beta 0.02, alpha Sharpe -0.62. One extra day of lag shrinks the loss to -0.17, so the loss is concentrated right after the signal, the signature of short-term reversal: high up-volume share is also high recent up-move intensity, which reverts.
Learned: The up-volume footprint, measured from daily closes, picks up short-term reversal rather than order splitting. Same lesson as 003: daily volume signals in this universe are contaminated by the price move that comes with the volume. Not flipped. A cleaner test would need intraday or signed-trade data the judge doesn't have, so I'd drop this idea.

## 015 correlation-peer momentum (discovery: scored, Sharpe 0.32, t 1.1, DSR 0.02)
Category: novel.
Hypothesis: At each month-end, for every universe stock find its ten most correlated peers using the past 252 days of daily returns net of the equal-weighted market; score the stock by its peers' average return over the last 21 days (own return excluded). Long top decile, short bottom decile, dollar-neutral, monthly.
Case: Slow diffusion of news across linked firms is documented with explicit link data (industry momentum, Moskowitz & Grinblatt; customer-supplier momentum, Cohen & Frazzini), and the explanation (limited attention, analysts covering one firm but not its links) should apply to any economic link. Residual return correlation is a data-only way to recover those links, including links that industry codes miss, which is the novel part. Excluding the stock's own return keeps the signal clear of its own one-month reversal. The strongest reason it might not work: in the most liquid stocks, cross-firm news is priced within days by sector ETFs and stat-arb desks, so a one-month horizon may be far too slow; and correlated peers that moved together last month may share a common short-term reversal, making the signal reversal rather than momentum.
Result: Net Sharpe 0.32 (gross 0.46), beta -0.14, alpha Sharpe 0.41, max DD only -23%, costs 1.8%/yr. t 1.1, far below the noise bar (0.96). With one extra day of lag it turns to -0.17; on a result this weak that swing is noise by the skill's rule, but it hints that whatever is there is fast, i.e. not the slow monthly diffusion I argued for.
Learned: Data-derived peer links give a small, market-neutral, positive-signed spread with shallow drawdowns, but nothing distinguishable from noise in one block. The idea is not dead; a shorter horizon (weekly peer return) would be a different hypothesis (fast lead-lag) and should be argued as such, not tried as a parameter tweak.

## Session summary (Season 1, proposer claude-opus-5-5, candidates 001-015)
Tried: 15 strategies, one judge run each in discovery, no reruns (no file errored or was rejected by the checks). Categories: research 001-003 (MAX effect, return seasonality, high-volume premium), popular-online 004-006 (RSI(2) pullback, MACD state, sell in May), pattern 007-009 (gap-down reversal candle, relative breakout on volume, wide-range strong close hedged), numeric 010-011 (composite rank, residual z-score reversal), market-regime 012-013 (breadth timing, momentum/low-vol switch), novel 014-015 (quiet accumulation, correlation-peer momentum).
Validation: none asked. No candidate met all the thresholds (DSR at least 0.9, t above 2, net and alpha Sharpe above the noise bar, expected sign). The best DSR was 0.30 (006).
What came out: three academic anomalies failed in this liquid universe, two of them with the opposite sign (002 significantly so, t -2.3). Every fast single-stock rule (004, 007, 009, 011) had some gross edge or none, and costs or beta took it; daily-reweighted event books and a daily-resized hedge (009) are cost traps. Every long-only stock-selection book ended up with beta near or above 1 and alpha near zero, except low-vol tilts. The rules that improved risk-adjusted returns are all slow exposure or style timing: 006 sell in May (0.58), 012 breadth (0.54, max DD -27%), 013 momentum/low-vol switch (0.61, t 2.0). All three rest on a handful of switches per decade, so they are weak statistically.
Worth a human's attention: nothing as a finding. If anything, 013 is the one to pre-register for a future season as a single run (it beat both ingredient baselines), but its large sensitivity to one extra day of lag and -55% drawdown argue it is fragile. 015 is a reasonable market-neutral idea with shallow drawdowns that needs a sharper hypothesis about horizon before another trial.
Notes for the next proposer: the noise bar rose from 0.34 to 0.97 over this session, partly because strongly negative trials (002, 009) widen the spread of Sharpes across trials. Avoid cost-driven failures (they inflate the bar for everyone) by checking turnover in the construction before running.
